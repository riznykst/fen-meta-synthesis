Getting started

What is this system, in one paragraph?

FEN × Meta is a human-governed validation layer for knowledge extracted from documents by an AI model. The model (Meta muse-spark-1.3) reads two or three source documents and proposes structured claims. Deterministic code — no model involved — checks that every quoted piece of evidence really exists in its source. A human reviewer then accepts, rejects, or disputes each proposal. Only that human decision creates a final status, and every decision is recorded with its full provenance: source document, exact quote, model and prompt, reviewer, verdict, and time.

What is the fastest way to see it work?

Open the simplified demo, leave the pre-filled synthetic documents (or switch to the Sumerian dictionary entries), and click Analyse documents. You will get proposals labelled “Proposal — awaiting human decision”. Click Show in source to see a quote highlighted in its document, then record an Accept on one proposal and a Dispute on the contradiction. The accepted item appears in the Registry with a downloadable record. The whole path takes about a minute.

How do I use my own documents?

Three ways, all equivalent: paste text into the document fields; click + Add third document for a third source; or use Upload document(s)… / drag-and-drop with .txt, .md, or .json files. A JSON file shaped as {"documents":[{"title": "...", "text": "..."}]} expands into several documents. Files are read in your browser and placed into the fields — nothing is sent anywhere until you click Analyse. Limits: at most 3 documents and 30,000 characters in total (the same limit the analysis API enforces). Over-limit text is rejected with an explanation, never silently truncated, because truncation would break quote verification.

What do the dataset buttons do?

Demo (synthetic) loads an invented neutral example, labelled synthetic so it can never be mistaken for corpus data. Sumerian dictionary entries (ePSD2) loads short, verified dictionary excerpts for the candidate lemmas lu, bahar, and me. They are dictionary excerpts — not corpus records and not gold evaluation data. Gold data is created only from attested source texts selected and verified by a human.

The workflow, step by step

  1. Sources. Two or three documents, each identified (d1, d2, d3 in the demo).
  2. Model proposal. The Meta model returns structured JSON: claims (each with a verbatim quote, the claim as stated, and the model's interpretation kept separate), ambiguities, an uncertainty level, a review recommendation, and contradictions with alternative interpretations.
  3. Deterministic checks. Code validates the contract, checks that every quote can be located in its document, that contradictions reference real claims, and that identifiers are unique. The model cannot influence this step.
  4. Human review. You read the proposal next to its evidence and record Accept, Reject, or Dispute, optionally with a comment. Your name in the Reviewer field is recorded with the decision (attribution, not identity verification).
  5. Record. The decision is appended to the registry. Accepted knowledge is showcased in the Registry section and can be exported as a JSON-LD record carrying the whole chain.

The verdict buttons — what each one means

ButtonWhat you are sayingWhat happens next
Accept “This claim faithfully represents its source, and I take responsibility for it.” Status becomes Accepted. The item enters the Registry as accepted knowledge and can be downloaded as a JSON-LD record. Available only when the quote is verified — otherwise the button is disabled and the API returns 409.
Reject “This must not become knowledge” — wrong, invented, or not supported by this source. Status becomes Rejected. The record is not deleted: it stays in the decision history and provenance with your comment, so it remains visible that the community already considered and rejected it. It never enters the Registry.
Dispute “I disagree or I am not sure — this needs discussion or an expert.” Status becomes Disputed. There is no final outcome: the item stays open for another reviewer or an expert. This is the normal verdict for contradictions between sources.
How do I read the “Decided proposals” list?

Every line is a decision that was already recorded (not a button), in one format:

[Final status] · [reviewer] · [proposal id] — [your comment] · [time, UTC]

Example: “Rejected by a human reviewer · reviewer-1 · c3 — Plurality was expressed by reduplication…” means proposal c3 was rejected with that comment. A contradiction such as x1 marked Disputed was deliberately left open instead of being decided on the spot.

Can I change my mind after deciding?

Yes — decide again on the same item. Decisions are immutable and append-only: the new verdict becomes a new record, the old one stays in the history untouched, and the item's current status is always the latest decision. Nothing is ever edited or silently overwritten.

Why is there no “Edit proposal” button?

Deliberately. A proposal is the model's output; editing it would destroy the audit trail (nobody could later tell what the model said versus what a human wrote). If a proposal is almost right, reject or dispute it with a comment explaining the correction, or submit a corrected document and analyse again — both actions stay visible in the record.

The architectural rule of the whole system: the model only proposes. No code path can assign accepted without a recorded human decision behind it. This follows the project's existing decision ADR-004.

Quotes and verification

What exactly is verified about a quote?

That the quote can be located in the referenced source document: an exact substring match, or a match after whitespace is normalised (so line breaks and multiple spaces do not matter). The check is deterministic code; the model is not asked and cannot influence it. Quote verification proves traceability — the words are really in the source. It does not prove the claim is true, and it does not prove the model understood the quote correctly. Judging meaning remains your job as the reviewer.

What does the red card mean?

The proposal's quote could not be located in its source — the classic case of a model inventing evidence. The card is shown red, states “Quote NOT verified”, and Accept is disabled. The API enforces the same rule server-side (409), so bypassing the button in the interface does not help. You can still Reject or Dispute the item — that is usually the right response to an invented quote.

What does “Show in source” do?

It opens the proposal's source document with the exact quote highlighted. Use it before deciding: it is the fastest way to see the evidence in context instead of trusting the card. If the quote cannot be located, the panel tells you so — that is precisely why acceptance is disabled for that item.

Why does my valid quote show as unverified?

Usual causes: the quote was edited in the document field after analysis (re-run Analyse); punctuation or case differs (matching normalises whitespace, not punctuation); or the quote spans text that the model joined from two places. Check with Show in source, then either fix the document and re-analyse, or Reject with a comment.

Contradictions between sources

A contradiction card appears when the model thinks two claims may conflict. It shows both claims side by side with their quotes, a kind (for example temporal_mismatch, value_mismatch, direct_negation, or other), the model's explanation, and alternative interpretations — readings under which the conflict would dissolve (“the dates may refer to different events”, and similar).

How should I decide a contradiction?

Dispute is the default: you are recording that the sources disagree and a person with domain knowledge must resolve it. Accept on a contradiction means “yes, this is a genuine conflict worth keeping as a documented disagreement” (still only possible when the involved quotes are verified). Reject means the model invented the conflict — for example it misread one of the quotes, which you can check with Show in source on both claims.

Dictionary badges (ePSD2)

On proposals that name one of the verified Sumerian candidate lemmas, the card shows a green dictionary badge, for example: Dictionary (ePSD2): lu [PERSON] (N) · person; man · 18,895 attestations (CC BY-SA 3.0), linking to the dictionary entry.

What does the badge prove — and what does it not prove?

It proves the lemma exists in the electronic Pennsylvania Sumerian Dictionary with that gloss and that many corpus occurrences. It is orientation metadata for the reviewer. It does not verify the proposal's quote (that is the separate quote check), it is not evidence for the claim, and it never counts as a vote.

Why are there two badges for “me”?

Because ePSD2 has two different headwords written me: me [BEING] (noun — Being, divine properties) and me [BE] (verb — to be). The badge follows the gloss in the proposal so the two are never merged. The same distinction applies in the lemma candidate register: noun and verb are separate entries.

Why is grammar (cases, pronouns, prefixes) not in the lemma data?

By design. Case endings, pronouns and verbal affixes are grammatical markers, not lemmas. Recording them as dataset lemmas would add noise to lemma-extraction evaluation. They belong in grammar references, not in the candidate register.

Provenance, the Registry, and record export

What does the Provenance view show?

The complete chain for one item: the source document, the exact quote, the model proposal (model name, SHA-256 prompt hash, timestamp), and every human decision in order (reviewer, verdict, comment, time). If you can see this chain, you can audit how the claim got its status without trusting anyone's summary of it.

Where does accepted knowledge actually go?

In this demo build: into the Registry. Accepted items are listed in the Registry section with their statement, quote, reviewer, and time, and each can be downloaded as a JSON-LD record (GET /records/{item_id}) containing the claim, quote, source documents, model and prompt hash, and all decisions — the artefact that a knowledge graph can later ingest unchanged. When the demo is started via scripts/start_demo.py, analyses and decisions are also persisted to an append-only local log (artifacts/store.jsonl), so restarting the demo does not lose your verdicts. Writing decisions into the baseline Kafka → RDF governance pipeline exists in the underlying research prototype but is deliberately not claimed as wired into this hackathon workflow.

What is in the downloaded JSON-LD record?

A gfen:ValidationRecord: item and analysis identifiers, current status, the claim object (quote, statement, interpretation, uncertainty), source documents, the model proposal metadata (model, prompt hash, creation time), and the full decision list. It is designed to be self-contained: a third party can verify the chain from the file alone.

Why can I not download a record for a proposal I have not decided?

Because a record is a decision artefact. The API returns 409 for an undecided item: a model proposal on its own must never be exportable as if it were validated knowledge.

Candidates & community voting (classic portal)

The classic portal and Triadic view are the pre-hackathon baseline demo: candidates are voted on by a simulated community DAO. Their outcome buttons mean the same as the Review verdicts:

Candidates buttonEquivalent verdictMeaning
validatedAcceptInclude this entity as community-validated.
disputedDisputeFlag disagreement; needs discussion or experts.
rejectedRejectDo not include; the rejection stays on record.
What should I read before voting on a candidate?

Click “Details — what am I voting on?” in the candidates list (or in the Triadic consensus list). The detail page shows the entity, document, current votes and quorum progress, peer assessments with comments, the recorded decision if one exists, the governance registry record, an explanation of each outcome, and the same voting buttons — so you can decide on the page where you read the evidence.

What are quorum, QV intensity, cost, and delegation?

These belong to the simulated DAO mechanics of the baseline demo. Quorum: in community mode a candidate is decided when enough votes arrive and the majority wins. Quadratic Voting (QV): a vote has an intensity of 1–5 and costs intensity² credits, so strong opinions are possible but expensive; a decision is taken when one outcome's weighted score reaches the threshold. Delegation (liquid democracy): a voter who has not voted can delegate their weight to another voter, and it follows that voter's choice. These mechanisms are a simulation for the demo (ADR-005); production governance lives outside this repository.

What are the small export links (ttl, jsonld, nt, crate)?

They download the candidate's governance record from the registry in different formats: Turtle, JSON-LD, N-Triples, or an RO-Crate package. They are read-only exports of what was decided, not editing tools.

Data, privacy, and licences

Where does my uploaded text go?

Uploads are read in your browser into the document fields. Text is sent to the model service only when you click Analyse, as the analysis request. API keys are never entered in the interface and never appear in responses, records, or logs; they come from environment variables on the backend machine only.

Which data in the demo is real, synthetic, or recorded?

Labels are explicit everywhere: synthetic documents and fixtures are invented neutral examples; the Sumerian material is short dictionary excerpts from ePSD2 for verified candidate lemmas; real corpus texts and gold evaluation data are supplied and licence-checked by a human and are never generated by the agent. Model responses used in tests are marked SYNTHETIC or RECORDED in their fixture files.

Can I reuse the Sumerian dictionary material?

ePSD2/Oracc data is licensed CC BY-SA 3.0: reuse is allowed with attribution, and substantial reuses must keep the same licence. The canonical attribution is recorded in eval/dataset/SOURCES.md together with the other sources' terms (notably: ETCSL texts are not openly licensed and are not republished here).

Glossary

TermMeaning in this system
ClaimOne atomic statement extracted from a source, carried by an exact quote.
QuoteThe verbatim evidence span copied from the source document; the unit of verification.
InterpretationThe model's reading of a claim, stored in a separate field so it can never masquerade as the source's words.
ProposalAny model output before a human decision. Proposals have no authority.
ProvenanceThe auditable chain: document → quote → model proposal → human decision(s).
Prompt hashSHA-256 of the exact prompts that produced a proposal, so the run can be reproduced and audited.
LemmaA dictionary headword (for example lu [person]), independent of its spellings in texts.
AttestationA real occurrence of a form in a corpus text, with a citable source.
Gold dataHuman-made reference answers for evaluation. Agents never generate gold data here.
B0 baselineThe share of proposals with unverified quotes — what would have passed as trustworthy without the verification gate.
RegistryThe store of recorded decisions and accepted knowledge (JSONL-backed in the demo launcher).

Limitations — read this before trusting the demo

Quote verification proves that words exist in a source, not that a claim is true and not that the model understood them. Reviewers still judge meaning. Contradiction detection depends on the model and is so far measured on a small, clearly labelled synthetic set; corpus measurements follow the human-supplied dataset. The DAO, voting, reputation, and ledger anchors of the baseline are simulations. The hosted website is a static interface; the model backend runs locally.

Rule that never moves: the model only proposes. accepted exists only after a recorded human decision.
